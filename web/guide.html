What YPEA Studio is

YPEA Studio, by Yarpiz, is a free app that runs in your browser. You describe an optimization problem in forms: the numbers it uses, what may change, what to make as small or as large as possible, and the limits a solution must keep. The app then searches for a good solution with a metaheuristic, which the app calls a solver, and shows the result in the terms of your problem.

You do not need to program, and you do not need to know the solvers to get a first answer. When you want more, you can compare solvers over repeated runs, share a project with a colleague, or continue in Python with the ypea package.

What stays on your computer

Everything. Your browser downloads the app from this site, and from then on all the work happens inside the browser. Nothing you type, import or compute is sent anywhere. There are no accounts and nothing to install.

  • Your projects and their results are kept in the storage of this browser, on this computer, and nowhere else.
  • Changes are saved as you work. The header of an open project says "All changes saved" when the last change is stored.
  • Another browser or another computer does not see your projects. To move one, download its file and open it there.
  • Clearing the data of the browser deletes your projects. Download a copy to keep them safe: "Download project" in the header of an open project, or "Download all projects" at the end of the start page.
  • Every report, script, table and picture is made in the browser and saved to your computer.

A first project, from start to result

This walk uses the worked example "0/1 knapsack": 20 items, each with a weight and a value, and a bag that carries at most 50 kg. Which items give the largest total value? The data are synthetic.

Open the example

On the start page, under "Try an example", choose "0/1 knapsack". The example becomes a project of your own, so you can change anything in it. The project opens on its experiment, "Quick run". The sidebar lists the parts of the project. In a narrow window the sidebar is behind the menu button at the start of the header.

Look at the data

Choose "Data" in the sidebar. The project has one table, items, with two columns: weight in kg and value in $. The first 5 of its 20 rows are shown. Positions start at 0, so the first item is item 0 and the last is item 19.

Look at the variables

Choose "Variables". A variable is what the solver may change. This project has one, take. It holds "Yes or no values (0 or 1)" as a list of 20 values, one for each item: 1 means the item is taken and 0 means it is left.

Look at the objective

Choose "Objective". The goal is "Maximize", the unit is $, and the expression is:

sum(value * take)

Each value is multiplied by its 0 or 1 and the products are added up, which gives the total value of the items that are taken. Under the expression the app says "Checked: this gives one number." when the whole project has no problem.

Look at the constraints

Choose "Constraints". There is one, "Total weight". Its left side is sum(weight * take), its comparison is "at most (<=)", its right side is 50 and its unit is kg.

Test a candidate

Before any run, check that the problem says what you mean. Choose "Test a candidate", check the boxes of positions 0, 2 and 4, and press "Test this candidate". The app answers with an objective of 231 $ and a total weight of 18 kg, which is at most 50 kg, so the constraint holds and its breach is "None".

Now also check positions 1, 8, 11 and 17 and test again. The objective is 558 $, but the total weight is 72 kg. The constraint reads "No, broken", with the breach "Over by 22 kg (44% of the limit)". A wrong column or a wrong sign would show up here, in numbers you can check by hand.

Run

Press "Run", which is at the top of every part. Beside it the app says what will run: "Quick run: Genetic Algorithm, 20,000 evaluations, seed 1". An evaluation is one candidate that the solver tries. While the run goes on you see the best solution so far, and "Cancel" stops it. A cancelled run keeps nothing. This run is over in a moment.

Read the result

  • The status: "Best feasible solution found". Feasible means that every constraint holds.
  • The objective: 617 $ with seed 1.
  • The evaluations used, 20,000 of 20,000, and the time the run took.
  • The constraints, each with its value and its limit: the total weight is 50 kg, at most 50 kg, so it holds.
  • The solution: the 0 or 1 of every item, and a drawing that shows which items are chosen.
  • A chart of the best value against the evaluations used.

The result also says that it is one run of a randomized solver with a recorded seed. "Run again with a new seed" shows how much the answer moves from run to run. Under the result, "Download the report" and "Download the Python script" save the two files that are asked for most, and "All downloads" leads to the rest.

The workspace

The start page lists your projects, the worked examples, "New blank project" and "Open a project", which loads a project file from your computer. An open project is one screen:

  • The header has "All projects" (back to the start page), the name of the project, the saving state and "Download project".
  • The sidebar lists the parts of the problem (Overview, Data, Variables, Objective, Constraints, Functions, Drawing), then "Test a candidate", the experiments, "Compare experiments" and "Share". A part that has a problem is marked there.
  • The main area edits the part that is selected.
  • The run bar stays at the top: the "Run" button, the experiment it will run, and one line for messages. After a run that you started from another part, "Back to" returns you to that part.

A problem is shown next to the field that causes it, and the Overview part says what to fix first. A project that still has a problem does not run, and the app shows the part that holds the problem.

Data

Data are tables of numbers that you import from CSV files. There are two buttons, because a header row changes what a file means:

  • "Import a table" reads a file whose first row holds the names of the columns. An expression uses a column by its name.
  • "Import a matrix" reads a file of numbers only, without a header row, such as a table of distances. An expression uses it by the name of the table.

The values in a file may be separated by commas, semicolons or tabs. Every cell must be a number, and a message names the line and the column of a cell that is not. The table takes its name from the file, and names are tidied so that expressions can use them. You can change the names, give each column a unit, and remove a column you do not need. Units are labels only. Nothing is converted.

Variables

Variables are what the solver may change. "Add a variable" adds one, and "What it holds" sets its kind. The fields that follow depend on the kind: limits, a total, or the number of items.

  • Decimal numbers within limits. One number, a list or a table, each value between a lowest and a highest value. For example the offset and the gain of a fitted line.
  • Whole numbers within limits. The same, with whole numbers. For example how many units to order.
  • Yes or no values (0 or 1). For example which items to take.
  • An ordering of n items. Every position from 0 to n - 1, each once. For example the order in which to visit cities.
  • k items picked out of n, in order. For example which 3 of 10 sites to visit, and in which order.
  • Exactly k of n switched on (0 or 1). A list of yes or no values with exactly k ones.
  • Ordered groups (n items in k groups). For the routes of several vehicles or the jobs on several machines. The order inside a group counts. Every group is alike.
  • Groups without order (n items in k groups), as a table of 0 and 1. Only the group of each item counts.
  • Shares of a fixed total. A list or a table of values that add up to a total you give.
  • A table in which every row adds up to a fixed total.
  • A table in which every column adds up to a fixed total.

Objective

The objective is one expression that gives one number, with the goal "Minimize" or "Maximize" and an optional unit. It can use the variables, the data and your functions by name, and sum, min, max, abs, len and ops. Arithmetic on a list works on every value of the list, so sum(value * take) needs no loop.

"Write the expression from a preset" writes the common ones for you: a weighted sum, the cost of a route, the total length of several routes, the total of selected items, the cost of an assignment, and the squared error of a straight line. What a preset writes is ordinary text that you can edit.

Constraints

A constraint is a limit that a solution must keep: a left side, a comparison ("at most", "at least" or "equal to") and a right side, with a name and an optional unit. "Add a constraint from a preset" writes a capacity limit or a limit on the largest load of a group. A project without constraints allows every candidate.

  • Tolerance. Only an "equal to" constraint has one. It is "Exact" at first, and the list offers "Within 0.1% of the right side", "Within 1% of the right side", "Within a fixed amount" in the unit of the constraint, and "Both, typed by hand". The rule is that the two sides may differ by the fixed amount plus the fraction times the size of the right side. A percentage of a right side of 0 allows nothing, and the form says so.
  • Turning a constraint off. Clear "Check this constraint" to turn a constraint off without removing it. Runs and the candidate test then skip it. The sidebar counts it as off, and every result says which constraints were not checked.
  • How a breach reads. Results show each constraint with both sides in its own unit, whether it holds, and its breach: the difference between the two sides, and that difference as a percentage of the limit, for example "Over by 22 kg (44% of the limit)". The word is "Over" for "at most", "Short" for "at least" and "Apart" for "equal to". The percentage is left out when the limit is 0.

Functions

When one expression cannot hold the logic, write helper functions. They are written in a small part of Python: def, assignment, if, for and return. A function gets the variables it needs as arguments, and positions start at 0. There are no imports, classes, while loops or strings, and text that the app does not support is refused with a message and its line number.

The complete rules are in the language rules in the repository of the app on GitHub.

Drawing

A drawing shows a solution as a picture, while a run improves it and in its result. Under "What to draw" you choose "A route through points", "Several routes from one depot", "A fitted line over data points" or "Which items are chosen", and then name the variable and the columns it uses. With "No drawing" a solution is shown as a table. For routes, an optional table of distances lets the drawing give the length of each route.

Test a candidate

Here you try one solution by hand before any run. Type the values, or use "Fill from the baseline" or "Fill with a random candidate", then press "Test this candidate". The app shows the objective value, every constraint with both sides, and the drawing. Modeling mistakes show up here, where they are cheap.

The same part stores the two comparison values of a project. Every result is compared with them when they are given:

  • "In use today (baseline)" is the solution you use now. "Store this candidate as the baseline" keeps it.
  • "Known answer (reference)" is an answer that is already known: a known objective value, a known solution, or both.

A result says "better" or "worse" only when both solutions keep every constraint.

Experiments and results

An experiment is one solver with a budget and a seed. A project can keep several, each with its own name, and each keeps the result of its last run. "New experiment" in the sidebar adds one: a copy of the experiment on screen, or, from any other part, one with the suggested solver. "Duplicate" also copies the one on screen.

  • Solver. This version has nine: Random Search, Particle Swarm Optimization, Differential Evolution, Success History based Adaptive Differential Evolution, Genetic Algorithm, Simulated Annealing, Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy, Nelder-Mead Simplex and Imperialist Competitive Algorithm. The settings of the chosen solver are one click away, under "Settings of" and its name.
  • Budget (evaluations). How many candidates the solver may try. The budget is the only measure of effort, so that solvers can be compared fairly.
  • Seed. The number that starts the random choices of a run. The same seed gives the same run again.

In a blank project the first experiment starts with a suggested solver, with one sentence that says why, and a budget of 20,000 evaluations. The suggestion follows your variables: Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy for decimal numbers only, Simulated Annealing for orderings, selections and groups, and the Genetic Algorithm for yes or no values, for whole numbers, and for a mix of orderings with other kinds. The app only suggests. Once you change the solver, a setting or the budget, the experiment is yours and the app no longer touches it. When a solver was designed for other kinds of values than your project has, a note says so. It is a note and not an error, because such a solver may still work well.

A result states its status ("Best feasible solution found" or "No feasible solution found"), the objective, the differences from the baseline and the reference when they are given, every constraint, the solution, the evaluations used and the time. When no feasible solution was found, the candidate that breaks the constraints least is shown and marked as not feasible. If you change the project after a run, the result says that it belongs to the definition as it was then.

Compare experiments

One run of a randomized solver can be lucky or unlucky. This part runs each chosen experiment several times, every run with its own seed and all with the same budget, and describes the runs side by side. A first result does not need it.

  • A comparison starts with 10 runs of each experiment. "20 runs" and "30 runs" are one click away, because papers in this field usually report 20 to 30. Any number from 1 to 100 can be typed.
  • One experiment is enough. Its runs are then compared with each other.
  • "Add Random Search as a reference" adds runs that only try random candidates, with the same budget, as a simple reference.
  • A comparison runs in the background, so you can keep working in the project. Leaving the project or closing the page stops it.

The summary gives, for each experiment, the number of feasible runs, the evaluations used, and the median, the first to third quartile and the best value over the feasible runs only. A chart for each experiment shows the median and the middle half of the runs against the evaluations. The summary only describes the runs. It names no winner, ranks nothing and prints no significance test, so the conclusion is yours.

Share

This part holds every file you can take away. Each one is made in the browser and saved to your computer.

  • The project, as one JSON file. "Download the project" holds the definition and every experiment. "Download the project with results" also carries the stored results and the comparison. A colleague opens the file with "Open a project" on the start page and can run it again.
  • Report. One HTML page with the result, its drawings and the definition that produced it. It opens in any browser and prints well.
  • Python script. One file that repeats the run with the ypea package.
  • Results as data. The result as JSON, and the solution and the history of the run as CSV.
  • Charts as pictures. The history chart and the drawing of the solution, as SVG and PNG. Pictures are saved in the light theme, so that they read well in a document.
  • The comparison. Its summary as CSV and its charts as SVG and PNG.

Undo, removing and renaming

  • Undo and redo. Every edit of the problem and of the setup of an experiment can be taken back with Ctrl+Z and made again with Ctrl+Y or Ctrl+Shift+Z (Cmd on a Mac). Inside a text field these keys undo your typing in that field, as in any form, so click outside the field first. Up to 100 steps are kept while the project is open. Runs and stored results are never undone.
  • Removing. Removing a table, a column, a variable or a constraint happens at once, and the message line of the run bar says so with an "Undo" button beside it. When other parts of the project use what you remove, a dialog first names each of those places. If you choose "Remove anyway", nothing in those places is rewritten. Each shows a problem until you change it.
  • Renaming. When you rename a table, a column, a variable or a function that other parts use, a line under the field says how many places still use the old name and offers "Rename there too". One click carries the new name to those places.
  • Deleting an experiment or a project asks first, because their results are deleted with them and that cannot be undone.

Repeating a run

Every random choice of a run comes from its seed, so a run can be repeated exactly.

  • The same project, solver, settings, budget and seed give the same run again. A colleague who opens your project file gets the same run too.
  • Each result stores the definition that produced it, with the solver, its settings, the budget, the seed and the versions. Later edits never change what a stored result refers to.
  • "Run again with a new seed" writes the next seed into the experiment, so the form and the result always agree.
  • In a comparison every run has its own seed, derived from the seed of the experiment, and the summary lists them all under "What ran: the setups and the seeds".

The exported Python script repeats the run outside the app, with the same solver, settings, budget and seed. It needs the ypea package (pip install ypea) and Python 3.12 or newer. This version of the app was tested with ypea %YPEA_TESTED%, and the first lines of every script name that release and the command that installs exactly it. The Python run follows the app closely: number for number with most solvers, and within the last digits with Covariance Matrix Adaptation Evolution Strategy or with functions such as exp and log.

Limits

  • A good answer, not a proof. Metaheuristics search well and often find very good solutions, but they do not prove that a solution is the best one possible. One run is one sample. Use "Compare experiments" before you rely on a number.
  • Everything runs on your computer. The speed of a run depends on your machine, a run stops when you leave the project or close the page, and very large problems belong in Python.
  • Data size. A project holds at most 200,000 data values.
  • A small part of Python. Expressions and functions use a limited syntax, and anything else is refused. A check or a candidate test that takes more than 5 seconds is stopped.
  • Budgets are counted in evaluations, not in seconds. There is no time limit for a run, only "Cancel".
  • One objective. A project has one objective. Units are labels and are not converted.
  • Browsers. The app is checked in Google Chrome, Microsoft Edge and Mozilla Firefox on a desktop computer, and the examples give the same results in all three. It has not been checked in Safari yet, and a run there may differ in its last digits. The layout adapts to a narrow window, but there is no separate design for phones.
  • Storage. Projects live in the storage of one browser. Download the ones you care about.

How to cite

Name the app, its maker, the version and the address, and add the day you used it. The version is printed under every result and in every report. This guide belongs to version %APP_VERSION%.

Yarpiz. YPEA Studio, version %APP_VERSION%. https://smkalami.github.io/ypea-studio/ (accessed on the day of use).

If your numbers come from the exported Python script, also name the release of the ypea package that ran it. For a result that others should be able to repeat, share the project file with results, which carries the seeds and the versions.

Roadmap

This is a list of directions, without dates and without promises.

In this version

  • The workspace: data, variables, the objective, constraints, functions, a drawing, the candidate test, and experiments with their results.
  • Six worked examples: Calibration line fit, 0/1 knapsack, Traveling salesman, Vehicle routing, Assignment and Benchmark functions.
  • Eleven kinds of variables and nine solvers. The Genetic Algorithm and Simulated Annealing can also work on orderings and groups directly.
  • Constraints with tolerances, constraints that can be turned off, and breaches in the unit of the constraint.
  • Comparisons over repeated runs, described and not judged.
  • Project files with or without results, the HTML report, the Python script, results as CSV and JSON, and charts as SVG and PNG.
  • Undo and redo, light and dark themes, and use by keyboard.

Planned

  • More solvers: Grey Wolf Optimizer, Teaching-Learning-Based Optimization, Harmony Search, Artificial Bee Colony, Ant Colony Optimization for continuous domains, Firefly Algorithm, Biogeography-Based Optimization, Bees Algorithm, Cultural Algorithm and Invasive Weed Optimization.
  • Solvers made for orderings: a discrete Ant Colony Optimization and Tabu Search.
  • A local improvement step for routes (2-opt).
  • Vehicles or machines that differ from each other.
  • Operators of your own for the solvers that work on the values themselves.
  • More worked examples, with scheduling among them.
  • A view of the moving population for problems with two decimal variables.
  • Checks in more browsers, with Safari first.

Being considered

  • A wider part of Python for expressions and functions.
  • A worked example of feature selection.
  • Links that share a small project without a file.
  • Statistical tests in a comparison.

Not planned

Servers, accounts, live collaboration, and export to languages other than Python.
